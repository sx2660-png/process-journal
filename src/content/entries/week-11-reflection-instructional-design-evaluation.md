## Takeaway

Calhoun, Sahay, and Wilson put evaluation in the middle of ADDIE, with a line back into every other stage. The questions they use are practical. Do the goals match the need. Do the materials and the assessments match those goals. Does the session that actually happens follow the plan. Did learners get the skill. Can they use it in the setting the design was for. A digital learning tool counts as instruction here, alongside a course.

Their map of what to look at is Kirkpatrick's four levels: reaction, learning, behavior, and results. Reaction is frequent feedback on how learners, and the teacher, take the new activity. The chapter treats engagement as a sign people will stay with the training. Learning asks whether the materials solved the problem that justified the design. Popham's transformative assessment is the version that follows a sequence of skills across the program, so mastery is a path. Behavior needs something you can score. Dick, Carey, and Carey want directions for what the learner does, plus a rubric, a checklist, or a rating scale, for the performance, the product, and the attitude. Results, following Morrison, Ross, Morrison, and Kalman, compare skills mastered with time spent, and also look at what the program cost to build, what it costs to keep running, how people react, and what benefit is still there later.

Timing splits into three. Formative evaluation happens while the design can still change. Summative evaluation happens at the end and asks, in Dick, Carey, and Carey's line, whether it solved the problem. Confirmative evaluation happens after implementation, on a repeating interval, to see whether the effect lasted. The picture they borrow from Stake is the cook tasting the soup, then the guests tasting it. Confirmative evaluation is the third taste: the guest liked it, and the question is whether they came back. Moseley and Solomon describe that third look as staying with what the stakeholders said mattered, and checking that those expectations are still being met.

Formative work, as Morrison and colleagues sort it, can be an expert review of accuracy and sequence, a decision the team still has time and money to change, a check against the objectives, a constructivist look at whether learners carried the skill into real life, or Patton's developmental evaluation, which stays loose enough to follow a design that is still changing. Tessmer's sequence is the one that tells you who is in the room. Expert review includes the designer, subject-matter experts, and often an outside reviewer. The target learners are absent. The checks are congruence, content, design, feasibility, and the user. One-to-one is closer to a usability test: clarity, impact, and feasibility. The learner talks through what they see, marks up the materials, and the session can be recorded. Krug's website testing is their model, down to watching the interaction later. Small group checks whether the revisions held. An instructor runs the lesson as designed. The designer watches and stays out of it, then a post-assessment asks what the instruction did. The field trial is the lesson in a setting as close as possible to the real one, with learners who match the people it is for, a finished instructor manual, and the designer again not teaching. The data are performance, attitudes, time, and whether the plan for managing the activity worked.

Summative models multiply once the product is fixed. Stufflebeam's CIPP sorts context, input, process, and product, for decisions still ahead and for accountability after. Stake's countenance model asks for a full description and then a judgment, across antecedents, transactions, and outcomes. Scriven's goal-free evaluation withholds the designer's objectives from the evaluator. Youker's rules are to find the effects without being handed the goals, decide what actually occurred, decide whether the program can be credited for it, and call the effect positive, negative, or neutral. The evaluator is a check on whether the change the designer wanted is the change that showed up.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

Our activity is the kind of product this chapter says the same tools cover. It is a short classroom tangible. After the early playtest we already cut it down: a fragile drum that was squeezed and hit until it broke, and the word "sustainability," which the children could not use. The version we are carrying forward is a tougher input, and a topic they can point at, electricity at home or exhaust from a car.

That playtest was an accidental one-to-one. Dick, Carey, and Carey would file the broken drum under feasibility. The input survived only when someone played it gently, inside the rule we had imagined. Clarity and impact were tangled up with that. We learned they would touch it, and we learned the object could not take the touch. Krug's step that we did not take is the recording. The squeeze lives in our memory of the session. A recording would let us separate a curious first hit from the hit that ended the object.

Kirkpatrick's levels do not all fit in the visit we are about to have. Reaction fits. Did they want to keep touching it. Did the teacher find it usable while teaching. Learning fits only in a thin way. A same-day question can ask whether a child can connect the input to a light left on at home, or to a car going by. Popham's progression needs a sequence of skills over time, and we have one meeting. Behavior is the level the chapter treats as transfer into the setting that mattered at the start. Turning a light off at home, or noticing exhaust on the way there, happens after they leave the room. A form that asks "what would you do" records an intention. Confirmative evaluation is the chapter's name for going back later to see whether the intention became the act. Results, in Morrison's sense, include time and cost. We have already used that level on ourselves. The interactions we cannot build in time are out, because a skill that never gets practiced has no efficiency to measure.

The form we draft for the day of the activity sits, in Tessmer's cycle, at the end of a small group or the start of a field trial. The chapter's field trial wants the instructor to run it, the manual to be finished, and the designers to watch. If we are still explaining the rules while the children play, we are inside the lesson, and the attitudes we write down will include their reaction to us. Scriven's goal-free pass is the uncomfortable one to add. An evaluator who had not been told our objectives might write that children tested an object until it failed, and that the talk in the room was about cars and houses. Youker would ask whether that effect was positive, negative, or neutral, without our learning goal in the room to settle it. Our goal calls the broken drum a failed input. The goal-free note calls it evidence of what the object taught them to do.

## Burning Question

The form for this visit can catch reaction, and a little learning, in the hour we have. Behavior and results, as this chapter defines them, wait until the soup is over and someone checks whether the guest came back. If we write every item from our objectives, we will also miss the effect Scriven wants on the page: the use we did not plan. I want to know which unplanned action we are willing to leave a blank for, the way the drum was an action we had not scored, and who reads that blank before we decide the activity worked.
