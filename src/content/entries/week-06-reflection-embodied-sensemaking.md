## Takeaway

Hummels and van Dijk start from a phenomenological claim: sensemaking is not a process that finishes inside the head and then gets reported. It is an ongoing coupling of brain, body, and the physical and social setting, maintained by sensorimotor loops and by the way people continuously adjust to one another. That claim changes what a tangible is allowed to be. A lot of tangible interaction still works in a Cartesian way. A physical object stands for a piece of digital information, and the hands are there to manipulate the symbol. The authors argue that this split sends design back to mind-over-world, even when the interface is made of wood. Their alternative is embodied sensemaking for face-to-face encounters: technology should sustain the coupling between people, so that shared insight emerges in the action rather than being stored and displayed. The seven principles are the design consequences of that claim. Cognition is socially situated, so the setting has to be a place people can actually use. Scaffolds and traces in the environment guide the next move. Ambiguous materials keep imagination moving. Dialogue is a coordination of empathy and curiosity, held open rather than closed into agreement. A first-person, bodily stake makes people responsible to each other. And an open catalyser, with no preset goal, can get strangers into physical contact fast enough for that coordination to start.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

The design-and-sensemaking studio in their Engaging Encounters project is where those principles become objects. The studio is for two people from different fields, sketching a future at the point where their practices meet, and it has to travel to them. The stage they were building is a wooden suitcase that opens into a standing table. People walk around it and pull in whatever the room already offers: a wall, a window, the things on a desk. That is social situatedness as furniture. The first versions failed in a way that makes the theory concrete. Fixed screens turned both partners toward the display and away from each other and from the stage. The next stage was a smaller curve, so the shared point of attention stayed in the center whether the two stood on one side or opposite each other. The fixed screen was then dropped. Digital material sits on separate iPods and iPads that can be picked up, passed, and put down like any other prop, next to sticky notes and rough physical prototypes. Those are scaffolds: temporary things in the shared space that steer the collaboration without becoming the subject of it.

The rest of the kit follows the same logic. Ideating in Skills is a set of tangible objects for acting a scenario out with the hands, using whatever connections the objects afford, rather than describing it. Inspiration and assessment cards and other ambiguous materials are there so a vague form can start a story. That is interactive imagery. Marks left in the action — a sketch, a row of objects — are traces: they record what happened and they also coordinate what happens next. Audio from each encounter is timestamped to RFID-tagged objects, so a placed thing can be used to return to the moment a shared insight actually occurred. The room itself is built like a workshop, with enough craft and enough trust that people will leave the comfortable version of their expertise and stay in the exchange. That is the dialogical system: the point is to keep adjusting to each other, through curiosity, and a consensus is not the success condition. Putting the body in, through physical meeting and through skill, is the first-person move. It lowers the barrier between disciplines because the contribution can be a gesture with an object, handled in public, rather than a credential. Each encounter opens with a catalyser called "We feel like talking," made by students Chris Gruijters, Janne van Kollenburg, and Kevin Andersen. Small magnets are attached to the fingers. An aluminum sheet stands between the two people, so they can feel each other's movement and cannot see it. The contact comes before the agenda.

## Burning Question

Antle and Wise, who we read alongside this paper, still give the physical object a representational job: a tangible can offload a mental task because the form maps onto the concept. PhonoBlocks does exactly that. The letter stands for a sound, and the color change tells the child the rule. Hummels and van Dijk would call that mapping a slide back into the Cartesian tangible, even though the child's hands are busy. I can see both. The children lining letters up and grabbing a blend with two hands were in a sensorimotor loop. They were also alone with a system that already knew the right answer. If our studio this semester is for people learning together, what would we have to give up — the correct mapping, the screen, the preset goal — before the tangible was supporting the coupling between them, rather than representing the content for them?
