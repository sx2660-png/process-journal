## Takeaway

Michelle Fine opens the assigned talk by refusing a slogan she had just heard repeated, including by people who fund schools: move fast and break things. She works with the young people whose things get broken, and her question is what an innovation leaves behind, and to whose lives it is accountable. The Public Science Project, where she is a founding member, has a blunt rule. They do no research that does not include, as co-researchers, young people who know what the injustice feels like. Expertise, she says, is widely distributed. Legitimacy is not. A superintendent once asked her for "student voice" on the achievement gap, for the fiftieth anniversary of Brown. She would do it only if the young people were researchers of the unfulfilled promise of integration, not quotations inside someone else's study. At the first research camp they told the adults they would stay, and they would not call it the achievement gap. It was an opportunity gap. The contact had to include people on the privileged side of the arrangement too, because a good school opening in one place is often a poor school closed in another. The product was not only a paper. Data went into dance and spoken word, so the finding could travel without leaving the particular schools behind.

Bautista, Bertrand, Morrell, Scorza, and Matthews show the same refusal as a method, inside the Council of Youth Research in Los Angeles. The Council is African American and Latino high school students from five schools, with teachers, graduate students, and professors. For eleven years they have researched the schools they attend and taken the findings to the people who run them. The paper's target is what counts as legitimate research: distanced, quantitative, "objective" work that documents racial disparities and never asks the students. Fine's line, which the paper quotes, is that the people most systematically excluded carry specific knowledge about how an unjust arrangement holds, and where it can break. Freire is the name they give to what happens next. Conscientization is not a feeling of awareness. It is learning to name the world, and then to pose problems rather than receive them. YPAR, as the Council practices it, puts that naming in the students' hands. They appropriate the ordinary tools — observation, databases, interviews — and they change what a finding is allowed to look like.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

In the summer of 2010 the Council met five days a week, about eight hours a day, on the tenth anniversary of the filing of Williams v. California. The 2004 ruling had made the state responsible for an "adequate" education. The students' job was to ask whether adequate, as Sacramento defined it, met what they actually needed. The topic split into teaching, curriculum, learning resources, leadership, and the social and physical life of the school. Adults taught methods and critical texts, then drove. The students videotaped, wrote field notes, passed out surveys, and pulled public statistics.

Sasha, from the school the paper calls South Central High, wrote up visits to three campuses. A teacher at the affluent one, Richside, told the group the school "didn't have much either." Sasha's notes list a planetarium, three cafeterias, and a new science and technology building. The authors add what the group walked through: an indoor court that retracted into an Olympic pool, and classes of no more than twenty. At South Central, teachers talked about furlough days and about ESL and special education staff who could not cover the students. At the third school the physical plant was the finding: dirty bathrooms, bars on the windows, fences around the campus, old computers. Sasha had chosen South Central because she had heard of worse schools and thought this one was good. After the visits she said that once you have seen the better school, it is not on the same level.

The group then tied those walks to a public number, the college opportunity ratio from UCLA's IDEA database. At the urban schools, fewer than 35 percent of students graduated. At the advantaged school, about 95 percent did. The college-ready rates split further. The observation without the ratio would have been a tour. The ratio without the walk would have been another disparity chart. Together they made a claim: urban students were not being prepared for college, and the reason was institutional, not a lack of ambition.

During the school year the South Central group studied learning resources, and they refused to start with the adults. Their question was what materials a student needs in order to be ready for college and for a life that matters. They interviewed students first, then built the teacher, counselor, and administrator protocols out of what those students had said. One result was a distinction the adults had not handed them: resources are physical (textbooks, computers) and they are also the other kind (a teacher who will hear a personal problem, a counselor with a caseload that allows a conversation, a building that feels safe). Questions to administrators were accountability questions: why teachers have to write their own grants for laptops, what the school is doing about students who have nowhere to take a personal problem. Their claim, in the paper's wording, is that students at the school lack quality technology and culturally relevant textbooks, and that this makes them less able to compete for college and less able to contribute to their community.

The finding did not stay in a journal. One slide drew the usual triangle, Sacramento officials at the wide top and students at the point. In the demands, the triangle is flipped: students, parents, and teachers are the wide top, and the officials are the point. A federal definition of a "highly qualified" teacher sits next to a classmate's sentence: an ideal teacher is one who listens, and does not only talk and read from the book. The Council's own definition adds what the credential leaves out. Fully credentialed, and emotionally and personally supportive, and teaching by problem-posing that is culturally relevant. A documentary puts a voice over two kinds of campus. Affluent schools "are able to access what they are entitled." Then: poor students of color "only seem to have access to this," and the image is black plastic taped to a wall with duct tape. A student's rap is not treated as data about youth culture. It is one of the ways the finding is said. Three students thank their parents, in Spanish, as their first teachers.

## Burning Question

Fine would not collect "student voice" for a study whose name the students rejected. The Council's adults still taught the methods, drove the vans, and co-built the slides. The paper is frank that the pushback, when it came, came from adult audiences, and that the students could stand in it because they stood together. Our own project is smaller and shorter, and the problem often arrives already named: a learning goal, a partner's brief, a technology we know how to build. If the young people who would use the thing refused our name for the problem, the way Fine's researchers refused "achievement gap," what part of the prototype would we be obliged to throw out, and who would be in the room when we decided?
