## Takeaway

The three readings land on one claim. Benjamin reminds us that "empathize" is the first move in the five-step method (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test) that now passes for human-centered design, and that the human who gets prioritized is usually the one paying the designer's bill. Once design thinking becomes the brand name for any creative act, the professional designer is cast as the person with agency. Vinsel's question makes that concrete: would design thinking have helped Rosa Parks "design" the Montgomery bus boycott? Irani and Silberman watched the same split happen to Turkopticon. They built the tool with crowd workers, in solidarity, and still found that public accounts gave the designers the plan and pictured the workers as people without one. Bennett and Rosner show the intimate version of this in disability design. In the name of empathy, nondisabled designers often try on a slice of impairment — a blindfold, a few minutes in a wheelchair, gloves that stiffen the hands — and then design from the shock of that exercise. What gets lifted out is the deficit: limited sight, limited movement. What drops away is the rest of a life, including the skill and invention people already use to get through an ordinary day. The exercise becomes a small spectacle, and the designer's discomfort becomes proof that they are compassionate. Bennett and Rosner call the alternative *being with*: staying with how someone actually lives, instead of making a short visit to their experience in order to know them.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

Japan's care-robot programs are the case I keep next to that argument. An aging population and a shortage of care workers gave the government and robotics companies a problem they could fund: not enough staff. The answer was machines. James Wright spent months in a nursing home trialing three of them. Hug, a lifting robot from Fuji, was meant to spare workers' backs. Pepper, a humanoid rented from SoftBank, was meant to lead the afternoon exercises so staff could be elsewhere. Paro, the robotic seal, was meant to offer animal therapy and to occupy residents with dementia who kept asking for attention. In the room, each one asked for more human care than it replaced. Residents could not follow Pepper on their own — it was too short to see, and its voice was too high — so a worker stood beside it, copied the movements, and repeated the instructions. The song list was small. After a few weeks people were bored, and Pepper came out less often. Staff resisted Hug because lifting was already a form of tactile, joking contact that gave both the worker and the resident *anshin*, a sense of ease. A machine in that moment felt disrespectful. One resident grew so attached to Paro that the attachment itself became something staff had to watch. All three had to be wheeled out, booted, cleaned, charged, explained, and put away. The residents had not asked for a robot companion. They were already asking for a person. A technology built for a policy problem and a product line had arrived looking for a scene that would justify it.

PhonoBlocks is the design that starts from the other end, and it is almost plain. Fan, Antle, and their colleagues worked with seven- and eight-year-olds at risk for dyslexia who had not learned six English letter-sound rules from ordinary classroom teaching. The system is a platform of seven slots and 46 hand-sized 3D letters. A notch under each letter means *b* and *d* only sit in the slot the right way up. Several children checked that notch, or held *b* and *d* next to each other, instead of storing the difference in working memory. The color is in the letter, not only on the screen. In the magic-*e* activity the vowel flashes yellow for the short sound and turns red, together with the *e*, when the long sound takes over. One child, building *car*, saw *a* and *r* turn red together and said they belonged as a pair before anyone explained the code. Children also moved the spelling out of their heads and onto the table: lining letters in alphabetical order so they could find them, gathering every letter for *trip* in one hand before placing them, picking up *s* and *h* with both hands at once once the blend was clear. A mental search became a spatial arrangement. Eight children improved on words they had practiced and on new ones — reading scores rose from about 19 to about 33 out of 36 — and they were still using the rules a month later. The mechanism was already in the tutoring tradition they drew on. Orton-Gillingham has children trace letters so the shape is in the hand. PhonoBlocks aims that practice at rules these children had already failed to learn. It does not borrow a child's impairment for the designer's inspiration. It gives the child something to do with their hands.

## Burning Question

PhonoBlocks still has designers, a facilitator, and a CHI paper. The children got better at the rules, and the researchers remained the authors of the intervention. Bennett and Rosner's *being with* asks for a slower relationship than a twelve-session study, and Benjamin's point about the bill still stands: a research prototype does not have to return venture capital, a product does. If the same letters had to scale across school districts and satisfy a funder, I am not sure the notch and the color change would be what survived. When the person in the room and the person paying are not the same, what actually decides the design — the learning mechanism, the relationship, or the bill?
