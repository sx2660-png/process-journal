## Takeaway

Antle & Wise reframed how I think about TUI design — every design choice (how immediate the feedback is, how the physical action maps to an outcome) is quietly a claim about how learning works. That means you can't make good design decisions without first being clear about your learning theory. Cooper et al. reinforce this from the research side: you also can't pick the right scaffolding until you've genuinely observed your users, not just assumed what they need.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

This reminded me of an internship where my team built an onboarding flow for new employees. We were convinced that people needed more information upfront, so we designed a detailed step-by-step guide without talking to a single user first. After launch, almost no one read it. It wasn't until we sat down with a few new hires that we realized they didn't want to read at all — they wanted to just start doing the job and figure things out as they went. If we had done even a few quick interviews at the beginning, we would have built something completely different. That experience made Cooper et al.'s point feel very real to me: skipping research doesn't save time, it just moves the problem later.

## Burning Question

When two relevant learning theories point in opposite directions — say, embodied cognition pushes for rich physical interaction while cognitive load theory warns against overwhelming the learner — how do designers actually decide which one to follow? Is there a framework for that, or does it always come down to judgment and experience?
