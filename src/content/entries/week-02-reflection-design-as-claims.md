## Takeaway

Antle & Wise made me realize that every TUI design decision — whether feedback is immediate or delayed, whether the mapping between physical action and digital outcome feels intuitive or arbitrary — is quietly making a claim about how learning works. Their five-element framework (physical objects, digital objects, actions on objects, informational relations, learning activities) isn't just a checklist; it's a tool for turning vague design intuitions into decisions that can be traced back to specific cognitive mechanisms. MagneTracks is a good proof of this: the magnetic tracks support artifact creation (constructionism), the challenge cards scaffold hypothesis-testing (constructivism), and the computer tracking system reduces cognitive load — three theories applied to three distinct design elements without conflicting.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

This connected directly to an internship experience where my team skipped user research and built an onboarding flow based on what we assumed new employees needed. We were confident in the design. After launch, almost no one engaged with it. It wasn't until we sat down with a few new hires that we realized they didn't want to read anything — they wanted to start doing the job and figure things out as they went. Cooper et al.'s point landed hard in retrospect: without research, you're designing for an imaginary user. The most ironic part was that the sections we spent the most time polishing were exactly the ones nobody needed.

## Burning Question

Antle & Wise survey five cognitive theories, but these theories sometimes point in opposite directions. Embodied cognition pushes toward rich physical interaction; cognitive load theory warns against overwhelming the senses. MagneTracks seems to navigate this, but the paper never surfaces how those trade-offs were resolved. When two applicable theories conflict, what's the designer's decision procedure? Is it driven by the learning objective, the age group, prior empirical evidence — or does it ultimately come down to the kind of value judgment that Hoadley & Cox would call phronesis?
