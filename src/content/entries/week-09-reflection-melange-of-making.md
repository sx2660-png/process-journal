## Takeaway

Bal, Nolan, and Seko start from a policy sentence and then take it apart. In 2010, at the NSF workshop Innovation, Education and the Maker Movement, Thomas Kalil of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy called maker culture the next phase of societal evolution. The account they use is Dale Dougherty's. Technologically, Neil Gershenfeld's personal fabrication is arriving. Economically, a maker ecosystem is forming in which people design it, make it, and sell it, and some of them become entrepreneurs. Kalil's education claim is that schools should put the tools of discovery, invention, and fabrication at every child's fingertips, in school and out of it, so those children can become DIY citizens. The makers he is describing find tinkering, inventing, and sharing rewarding in themselves. Do It Yourself and Do It With Others are part of who they are.

The chapter treats that as several cultures sharing a name. Holtzman, Hughes, and Van Meter call DIY fluid, constantly rewritten by the people in it, and describe it as a two-step: first a claim about value, then a claim about social relationships. It undercuts exchange-value and builds use-value outside capitalism. Bal, Nolan, and Seko set a paradox next to that definition. The same ethos has been absorbed into industry. At one end, DIY is a hobby market for consumers. At the other, people are constructing their own social realities, and hands-on production is how they get to self-determination, shared knowledge, and a community. Open source is their example of the second end already living inside institutions. It began among university people who wanted source code public, and who organized production by sharing, barter, and reciprocity. Marcin Jakubowski's Open Source Ecology, and the Global Village Construction Set, pushes the claim further: spread the means of production, and a lot of what looks like scarcity starts to look arranged. Shah and Tripsas call the people who turn this into a livelihood user entrepreneurs. Informal learning, which Livingstone defines as any pursuit of understanding that happens outside the curricula and the workshops of educational institutions, gets reorganized into what Brandenburger and Nalebuff called co-opetition. Cooperation becomes a way to compete.

What they want from that history, for children, is a narrower pair of capacities. Standardized schooling, as they describe it, shuts out hacking and autonomy, and those are the two pieces critical making needs. The hacking they mean comes with an ethic. Himanen's hackers are enthusiastic programmers who share their work, and who try to hold onto privacy and equality as life gets more technological. Tim Jordan's definition is the one the argument stands on. A hack alters a pre-existing situation so that something new comes out of it, and the alteration draws other people in to work together and to pass on what they learned. Kulikauskas separates that stance from the architect's. The hacker works bottom-up, on the special case, practically, without a curriculum for it, by solving the problem in front of them and building on what already exists. The architect works top-down, on the general case, from a master plan, and starts from scratch.

The distinction they then make about autonomy is the part I will keep. One kind they call auto-control. A person has internalized a group's norms so completely that they regulate themselves, and what they produce reproduces the group. In a marginalized community that can mean carrying its economic values forward. Inside a company it can look like initiative: a new niche, fitted to a local culture, that grows the market. Self-determination begins when someone has challenged a traditional norm and can see themselves as distinct and, at the same time, in relation to other people. Only from there, the chapter argues, does critical thinking get far enough to build a social reality of one's own. Critical making is that second autonomy, practiced on materials and on rules.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

Their picture of the child hacker is ordinary on purpose. A child builds a theory, changes one variable, and looks at the result. Physical knowledge shows up as throwing and dropping. Abstract knowledge shows up as comparing. Social knowledge shows up as learning how to speak, and how to get another person to respond. Making a mess, playing with food, making noise, and unstructured pretend play sit in the same list. Each one pushes against a room an adult has already arranged. The question they treat as the start of inquiry is "what would happen if?" From there a child can pose a problem, choose a next try, and begin the self-criticism that making needs. Doctorow's *Makers*, which they cite, is the adult scale of the same move: hack the hardware, the business model, or the living arrangement, so you can stay alive and happy when the economy fails.

The scene where they show adults misreading this is Sony's LittleBigPlanet. The game already sells itself as making. Players build a home, a pod, and worlds, called levels, out of objects and avatars. They explore, make characters and personal spaces, and play and talk with other players. A passing adult, the authors say, sees consumption of a corporate commodity. Sara Grimes's "The Hidden Playground" is the counter-scene they rely on. Children keep making their own social practices inside games and social media, and they do it most clearly when the environment blocks the interaction they wanted. The hack is a response to the block. The chapter's own study, "Voices from Digital Natives," is still preliminary here. What they report is that networked spaces let children use musical, visual, and social ways of knowing that formal school treats as a disadvantage, and that the identities, communities, and economies tried out in those spaces show up offline. Logan McCall's line, which they use to place this in a longer history, is that ordinary people have been craftspeople for as long as there have been tools, and that it is recent for the central tools of a life to arrive as products nobody can open.

The school projects are their attempt to keep the same hacking available after early childhood. From Gandini they take Reggio Emilia's culture of listening: the child's interest sits in the middle because the student is made visible and audible, and dialogue starts again around a culture of childhood that respects identity, potential, and rights. Beside it they name Gever Tulley's Tinkering School, the Edible Schoolyard, the School at Blair Grocery, and a 2010 New Orleans effort that was tying art to environmental work. The form they want is a school turned into a learning node inside a wider network of mobile learning commons. It would offer research infrastructure in the sciences and the professions that a neighborhood does not have, collaboration on a topic the learners chose, a route to people who already do the work, and community workshops aimed at a local problem. Delivering content, testing low-level recall, and running standardized evaluation would have to live somewhere else. The citation they hang the workshop idea on is Tulley's TED talk, "Five Dangerous Things You Should Let Your Kids Do."

They also say why this gets absorbed. A hidden curriculum, in Longstreet and Shane's sense, lives in the structure of the school and in how the staff already behave, and it keeps a new set of values from entering. A null curriculum, from Flinders, Noddings, and Thornton, is everything the school never teaches and never argues with. Leaving a value out tells students it matters less. The chapter treats the loss of self-worth that follows as damage to self-determination. Public elementary school, on their account, still teaches nineteenth-century middle-class values and industrial work protocols, and that training shrinks a learner's own sense of being. Even a staff that wants to change is not organized for curricular change. Parents have already started choosing other rooms. Two of the examples they give are still public: the Avon Maitland District e-Learning Centre, and the Algoma District School Board's support for homeschooling. They also argue that some governments are funding established DIY organizations to build educational infrastructure and to route around the school. A makerspace inside an unchanged school can train the auto-controlled kind of autonomy with better tools.

## Burning Question

Jordan's hack changes a situation that was already there. Kulikauskas's version builds on the thing in front of you. In our prototype, the situation already there is the task we wrote and the interaction we will know how to score. A child who ignores the task, rewires the object, or treats the material the way this chapter treats food and noise is doing the inquiry the authors call the first step of critical making. A completed activity is the outcome an auto-controlled autonomy is built to produce. I want to know, before we lock the interaction, what we will write down when a child alters the object, and who in the room gets to decide that the alteration counts.
