## Takeaway

Costanza-Chock's chapter asks for a shift that hiring campaigns never make. The Google memo fight, and most "diversity in tech" stories after it, stay on who gets the job. Design justice treats that as a first move and then keeps going, because white supremacy, heteropatriarchy, capitalism, and settler colonialism also run through the product: who is imagined as the user, whose needs survive economies of scale, who owns the platform, and who is credited when it ships. The unmarked user in that cycle is small and profitable — in the U.S. context, typically white, cis, straight, college-educated, English-speaking, online, and nondisabled. A more diverse team does not automatically change this. Unless identity is named, even mixed teams still picture that same default person. The usual substitutes make the absence look like a method. User personas, sometimes spun up by a generator that promises empathy "in far less clicks," often appear only at ideation, and too often they are the team's stereotypes given a name and a stock photo. Disability simulation has the same shape. A nondisabled person spends a few minutes in a wheelchair, lacks the skills disabled people have already built, overestimates the loss, and then designs a device that makes the body closer to a norm. What disabled people more often ask for is a change in the building. Costanza-Chock's line, taken from disability justice, is "nothing about us without us." In the U.S., participatory design has often been translated into a workshop that collects ideas and returns them as products. Input goes up. Accountability, ownership, profit, and credit stay with the professionals. At its best, design justice is closer to community organizing than to a consult.

## Connection to a Real-World Example

The Amparo case is the closest thing I have read to that handoff inside a school. Braz, Ramos, Benedetti, and Hornung worked for about a year with the Specialized Educational Service in Amparo, in the state of São Paulo. Four SES teachers, the municipal inclusion coordinator, and a pedagogic advisor sat with two HCI researchers from Unicamp. The problem they refused was a familiar one: a tangible built for one impairment and one activity. A stick for children with cerebral palsy, a ball game for one motor profile, a board only for students who do not speak. In a regular classroom that kind of object isolates the student, because their task is no longer the class's task. A teacher in the project put the practical version of the same failure in one sentence: a technology can be helping a student, and it still goes unused if the teacher cannot run it.

The researchers brought Makey Makey and Scratch, and low-tech copies of materials the teachers already used — a communication board, a cloth vest, a carpet — so the first encounters were in cardboard, Velcro, and Styrofoam rather than in a pitch. The teachers' first response was that these were only another way to run the activity they already ran, with no advantage. That refusal is where they became codesigners. Asked to design from the students they actually teach, they proposed a table with physical buttons and a tablet, a pedalboard for the feet, a wall-mounted device read by gaze, and activities with animation, because still paper and foam figures lose some students. The group kept the table and the pedals. Some students in the SES program have little movement in their arms; others have little movement in their legs. Five buttons on the tabletop would cover digits from zero to nine alongside a matching set of pedals; five pedals turned out to be too many for many students, so the pedalboard became four. The buttons were rebuilt for students with less strength. The laptop stayed, because schools already had one. A projector was added so the rest of the class could see the same activity, instead of the student working alone at a separate machine.

The evaluation that stays with me was one session with a six-year-old who has cerebral palsy. She uses a wheelchair, has limited movement in her arms, and does not speak; she answers with her face and her body. Her usual version of the task used a mouse switch: she could press it, and the teacher still had to park the pointer on the right choice. With the tabletop she matched animal drawings. The nearer buttons were available. She reached for the far ones, and when the teacher offered a closer button her face made the refusal plain. Afterward the teacher said she had acted alone, and that the freedom to choose was the rich part. The same teacher had said, early on, that when the researchers arrived she did not understand their language and could not imagine anything. The process that changed this was mutual and slightly awkward: when the researchers only facilitated and watched, the teachers felt judged; when decisions were made in the room, as equals, the teachers took more of the wheel. Two of the four authors of the paper are from the Amparo side, and the chapter keeps their wording instead of sanding it into one academic voice. That is a small, concrete version of the credit Costanza-Chock says usually never leaves the lab.

## Burning Question

The paper is honest about a limit I cannot settle. The students most affected by the table were evaluators, not codesigners. The authors' reasons are serious: the students' needs are so different that a few child-designers would have pulled the object toward one profile and away from Design for All; the teachers already spend intensive time with many of them; and SES teachers already design materials and then try them with students. Costanza-Chock's standard is stricter than "the closest professionals in the room." Deputy expertise is still a proxy. The researchers also chose Makey Makey before the teachers had a basis to choose, and built several prototypes between the short Friday meetings. Shared authorship moves credit. It does not yet answer who would own the table, the activity-authoring software, or the decision to replace the kit, once the research project ends. If "nothing about us without us" includes the child in the wheelchair, what would have to change in this process before she was a designer of it, and not only the person who reached for the far button?
